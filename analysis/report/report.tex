\section{Abstract}\label{abstract}

\textbf{Background.} The coefficient of determination \(R^2\) is
unambiguous in ordinary linear regression, but it admits no
single definition for linear mixed-effects models (LMMs). The
proliferation of competing \(R^2\) measures has left applied
researchers with limited guidance on which to report. Integrative
frameworks and earlier simulations exist. The finite-sample bias
of the widely used plug-in marginal and conditional \(R^2\), and the
effect of omitting the random-slope variance, have received less
attention.

\textbf{Methods.} We conduct an ADEMP-compliant Monte Carlo simulation\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-morrisUsingSimulationStudies2019}{1}}. It compares the original\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-nakagawaGeneralSimpleMethod2013}{2}} formula with the\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-johnsonExtensionNakagawaSchielzeth2014}{3}} random-slope extension.
The original formula uses the random-intercept variance only.
Both are implemented directly and checked against \texttt{MuMIn}. The
design is a 32-scenario factorial defined by the random-effects
structure (intercept-only, random-slopes), intra-class correlation
(ICC \(\in \{0.10, 0.40\}\)), target marginal \(R^2\)
(\(\{0.10, 0.40\}\)), and sample size (\(J \in \{20, 50\}\)
clusters crossed with \(n \in \{5, 20\}\) observations per cluster).
We evaluate each scenario at 500 replicates
(16,000 replicate data sets in
total) and
report performance measures with Monte Carlo standard errors
per\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-morrisUsingSimulationStudies2019}{1}}, including bias, relative
bias, MSE, empirical SE, monotonicity when a true or a null
predictor is added, robustness to an omitted random slope, and
the rates of singular fits and convergence warnings.

\textbf{Results.} The two estimators are identical for
random-intercept models by construction. Our Johnson estimates
matched \texttt{MuMIn} to machine precision. In random-slope models the
original formula overstated \(R^2_m\) (relative bias
1\% to 19\%) and
understated \(R^2_c\) (bias down to
-0.048). The Johnson \(R^2_m\) was biased
upward in small samples, with relative bias up to
12\%. An exploratory adjustment that subtracts
the sampling-variance term \(\text{tr}\{\text{Var}(\hat\beta) S_x\}\)
kept the absolute bias at or below
0.0072.
It slightly over-corrected in the smallest samples. Adding a null predictor
raised \(R^2_m\) in only 47\% to
77\% of replicates. Omitting a random slope
left \(R^2_m\) nearly unbiased but biased \(R^2_c\) downward by up to
0.085. Singular fits were common in
low-ICC random-slope scenarios\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-batesLme4LinearMixed2015}{4}}.

\textbf{Conclusions.} For random-slope models, the Johnson extension
should be used, as current \texttt{MuMIn} already does. Plug-in \(R^2_m\)
runs high when both the sample and the true \(R^2_m\) are small.
Changes in \(R^2\) after adding a predictor should not be read as
evidence about that predictor. We recommend reporting both
marginal and conditional \(R^2\), with measures of uncertainty when
\(J < 30\) or \(n < 10\), and with convergence diagnostics.

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

We begin with a basic question: how much of the variance in a
response does a model explain? The answer is a fundamental part
of scientific inference. In ordinary linear regression the
coefficient of determination \(R^2\) answers this question without
ambiguity. It is the ratio of the variance of the fitted values to
the total variance of the response. It also has a straightforward
reading as a goodness-of-fit measure. For linear mixed-effects
models (LMMs), however, no single definition of \(R^2\) has reached
consensus. The model contains both fixed effects and random
effects. As a result, the explained variance can be split in more
than one way. Unfortunately, the many competing measures have left
applied researchers with little guidance on which one to report.

This ambiguity has practical consequences. Mixed-effects models
are the standard analytic framework in many fields. These range
from ecology and psychology to clinical trials and educational
research\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-raudenbushHLM2002}{5},\citeproc{ref-snijdersMultilevelAnalysis2012}{6}}. When
investigators report \(R^2\) for an LMM, they rarely say which
definition they used. Nor do they discuss its sensitivity to
sample structure, ICC, and model complexity. We note that two
investigators may analyze the same data with different \(R^2\)
implementations. They may then reach substantively different
conclusions about model adequacy.

Several \(R^2\) measures for LMMs are available, and some have been
studied by simulation.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-lahuisExplainedVarianceMeasures2014}{7}}
evaluated several multilevel explained-variance measures by Monte
Carlo. Their set included the variance-of-predicted-values measure
that underlies the Nakagawa-Schielzeth approach.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-rightsQuantifyingExplainedVariance2019}{8}} later gave an integrative
framework. It treats ten earlier multilevel \(R^2\) measures as
special cases of a full variance decomposition. It also handles
random-slope variance explicitly.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-rightsNewRecommendationsUse2020}{9}} extend that framework to \(R^2\)
differences between nested models.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-piephoCoefficientDeterminationR22019}{10}}
offers a methodological commentary and worked examples.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-stoffelPartR2Partitioning2021}{11}} introduce the \texttt{partR2} package for
semi-partial variance decomposition. That is a distinct estimand.

Our contribution is narrower. We study the finite-sample behavior
of one estimator family in a small designed factorial with known
truth. The family is the plug-in marginal and conditional \(R^2\) of\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-nakagawaGeneralSimpleMethod2013}{2}}. We compare the original
random-intercept formula with the random-slope extension of\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-johnsonExtensionNakagawaSchielzeth2014}{3}}. We report bias, MSE and
monotonicity with Monte Carlo standard errors. The study follows
the ADEMP framework\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-morrisUsingSimulationStudies2019}{1}}. We do not
attempt to displace or benchmark against the broader set of \(R^2\)
proposals. Before
presenting the simulation design, we briefly review six principal
\(R^2\) definitions for LMMs. This places the two compared methods
in context. Only two of the six are evaluated empirically.

\subsection{R-Squared Measures for Linear Mixed Models}\label{r-squared-measures-for-linear-mixed-models}

The difficulty in defining \(R^2\) for mixed models comes from a
core ambiguity. But what counts as `explained' variance? In a
two-level model with cluster-level random effects, the fixed
effects explain variation in the outcome. So do the random
effects, which capture systematic between-cluster heterogeneity.
Should random-effect variance be treated as explained or
unexplained? The answer depends on the inferential goal. In this
section we shall review six approaches that resolve this question
in distinct ways.

\subsubsection{Nakagawa and Schielzeth (2013)}\label{nakagawa-and-schielzeth-2013}

\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-nakagawaGeneralSimpleMethod2013}{2}} proposed what has become the
most widely adopted framework. It distinguishes two quantities.
The \emph{marginal} \(R^2\) (\(R^2_m\)) is the proportion of variance
explained by the fixed effects alone. In symbols,

\begin{equation}
R^2_m = \frac{\sigma^2_f}{\sigma^2_f + \sigma^2_\alpha +
  \sigma^2_\varepsilon}
\label{eq:ns-r2m}
\end{equation}

where \(\sigma^2_f\) is the variance of the linear predictor (the
fixed-effect component). Here \(\sigma^2_\alpha\) is the
random-effect variance and \(\sigma^2_\varepsilon\) is the residual
variance.
The \emph{conditional} \(R^2\) (\(R^2_c\)) also counts the random effects
as explained variance. That is,

\begin{equation}
R^2_c = \frac{\sigma^2_f + \sigma^2_\alpha}{\sigma^2_f +
  \sigma^2_\alpha + \sigma^2_\varepsilon}
\label{eq:ns-r2c}
\end{equation}

This approach is implemented in \texttt{MuMIn::r.squaredGLMM()}\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-bartonMuMIn2024}{12}} and \texttt{performance::r2\_nakagawa()}\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-ludeckePerformanceAssessment2021}{13}}. On the plus side, it is
simple and easy to interpret. It also applies broadly to both
LMMs and GLMMs. The marginal/conditional distinction maps
naturally onto research questions about population-average versus
cluster-specific prediction. On the minus side, the original
formulation assumes a model with a single random-intercept term.
In models with random slopes, the variance of the random-effects
component depends on the distribution of the covariates. We note
that the original formula therefore needs modification in that
case.

\subsubsection{Johnson (2014)}\label{johnson-2014}

\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-johnsonExtensionNakagawaSchielzeth2014}{3}} extended the
Nakagawa-Schielzeth framework to random-slope models. The key
point is that random slopes make the random-effect variance
depend on the covariate values. That is, it is no longer a
scalar. Johnson replaces it with the mean, over observations, of
the random-effect variance at each design point. In symbols,

\begin{equation}
\sigma^2_{\alpha,J} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i,j}
  z_{ij}^\top \Sigma \, z_{ij},
\label{eq:johnson}
\end{equation}

Here \(z_{ij}\) is the random-effects design row. \(\Sigma\) is the
random-effects covariance matrix. For a random intercept and
a slope on \(x_1\) this is
\(\tau_0^2 + 2\tau_{01}\bar{x}_1 + \tau_1^2\,\overline{x_1^2}\).
The original formula uses \(\tau_0^2\) alone.

The Johnson extension keeps the marginal/conditional distinction.
It equals the original formula for intercept-only models. Its main
advantage is correctness for random-slope designs. On the other
hand, it shares a limitation with the original method. It treats
the random effects as a single composite and does not attribute
variance to individual predictors.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-nakagawaUpdatedArticleCoefficient2017}{14}} later revised the whole
framework. Their update adopts Johnson's random-slope variance. It
also adds the delta, lognormal and trigamma approximations for the
observation-level variance of non-Gaussian families. The trigamma
option therefore comes from the 2017 update, not from Johnson
(2014). Current \texttt{MuMIn::r.squaredGLMM()}\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-bartonMuMIn2024}{12}} follows
the 2017 update. It applies Johnson's term to every random-slope
fit. For a Gaussian LMM the observation-level variance is the
residual variance. The \texttt{method} argument then has no effect. A
user of current \texttt{MuMIn} thus always obtains the Johnson estimator.
For this reason we implement both estimators directly (see
Methods Under Comparison).

\subsubsection{Edwards et al.~(2008)}\label{edwards-et-al.-2008}

\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-edwardsR2StatisticFixed2008}{15}} proposed an \(R^2\) for the
fixed-effects portion of an LMM. It is built on an approximate
REML Wald \(F\) statistic for testing all fixed effects except the
intercept. The \(F\) statistic uses the Kenward-Roger small-sample
approximation. In symbols,

\begin{equation}
R^2_\beta = \frac{(q/\nu) F}{1 + (q/\nu) F},
\label{eq:edwards}
\end{equation}

with \(q\) numerator and \(\nu\) denominator degrees of freedom. The
statistic compares the full model with a null model. The null
model drops the fixed effects but keeps exactly the same
covariance structure. It therefore needs only the one fitted
model. The approach does not try to quantify random-effect
variance explained. It is implemented in \texttt{r2glmm::r2beta()}. The
Edwards statistic corresponds to \texttt{method\ =\ "kr"}. The package
supports it for \texttt{nlme::lme} fits. The package also offers the \texttt{sgv},
\texttt{nsj} and \texttt{lm} methods.

On the plus side, the approach is grounded in standard linear
model theory and focuses on fixed-effect inference. On the minus
side, it does not produce a conditional \(R^2\) analogue. Its value
also depends on the denominator degrees-of-freedom approximation.
A later likelihood-ratio variant for GLMMs is a different
statistic and is not reviewed here.

\subsubsection{Jaeger et al.~(2017)}\label{jaeger-et-al.-2017}

\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-jaegerR2glmmSemiPartialR22017}{16}} extended the Edwards et al.
framework by introducing \emph{semi-partial} \(R^2\) statistics.
These statistics decompose the fixed-effect \(R^2\) into
contributions from individual predictors or sets of predictors.
Their approach uses the Schur complement of the
variance-covariance matrix and the Wald statistic. Together these
quantify the variance attributable to each fixed effect. The
calculation accounts for the covariance structure induced by the
random effects. This is implemented in
\texttt{r2glmm::r2beta(method\ =\ "sgv")}.

Semi-partial \(R^2\) values are particularly useful when the
research question concerns the unique contribution of a specific
predictor, rather than overall model fit. The main
limitation is computational. The method needs inversion of
submatrices of the full variance-covariance matrix. As a result,
it can be unstable when there are many random-effect parameters
relative to the number of clusters. Like Edwards et al., it
addresses only fixed-effect variance and does not produce a
conditional \(R^2\).

\subsubsection{Xu (2003)}\label{xu-2003}

\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-xuMeasuringExplainedVariation2003}{17}} generalized \(R^2\) to LMMs as a
measure of explained residual variation for the whole model. Xu
described three families of measures. The first uses the
estimated variances directly. The second uses residual sums of
squares, as in linear regression. The third is based on
Kullback-Leibler information gain. The variance-based measure is
often written \(\rho^2\). It is one minus the ratio of the residual
variance to the total variance. In the population it equals a
conditional-type total \(R^2\)\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-rightsQuantifyingExplainedVariance2019}{8}}.

Level-specific measures are a separate line of work.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-raudenbushHLM2002}{5}} define a within-cluster and a between-cluster
\(R^2\). Each is the proportional reduction in a variance component,
relative to a null model. Two models are fitted. So these
measures can be negative in a sample\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-rightsQuantifyingExplainedVariance2019}{8}}.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-snijdersExplainedVariationMultilevel1994}{18}} proposed a related
level-1 and level-2 pair. Unfortunately, no widely used R package
provides Xu's measures directly. Implementation therefore needs
custom code.

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{The \texttt{performance} Package Implementation}{The performance Package Implementation}}\label{the-performance-package-implementation}

The \texttt{performance} package\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-ludeckePerformanceAssessment2021}{13}}
provides \texttt{r2\_nakagawa()}. This function implements the
Nakagawa-Schielzeth framework with the Johnson extension for
random slopes. The statistical method is the same as that of
\texttt{MuMIn::r.squaredGLMM()}. Both compute the fixed-effect variance
as the variance of \(X\hat\beta\). We checked this on 100 fits from
five of our scenarios, using \texttt{performance} 0.19.0
(\texttt{analysis/scripts/check\_performance\_agreement.R}). In the 84
non-singular fits, \texttt{r2\_nakagawa()} matched our Johnson estimator
to within \(10^{-14}\). The 16 singular fits behaved differently.
There \texttt{r2\_nakagawa()} returned no conditional \(R^2\). It also
returned a marginal \(R^2\) that ignores the random effects. That
value differed from ours by up to 0.046. The two packages
therefore differ in how they treat singular fits, not in the
estimator.

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{The \texttt{partR2} Package (Stoffel et al., 2021)}{The partR2 Package (Stoffel et al., 2021)}}\label{the-partr2-package-stoffel-et-al.-2021}

\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-stoffelPartR2Partitioning2021}{11}} implement \texttt{partR2}, which extends
the Nakagawa-Schielzeth marginal/conditional split to a
\emph{semi-partial} decomposition. That is, the marginal \(R^2\) is
further attributed to individual fixed effects or predictor sets.
The spirit is similar to Jaeger et al.~(2017). However, \texttt{partR2}
is built directly on the Nakagawa-Schielzeth variance
definitions. It is implemented for both LMMs and GLMMs, with
parametric-bootstrap confidence intervals. \texttt{partR2} postdates the
Nakagawa-Schielzeth/Johnson framework evaluated here. It is
compatible with that framework rather than competing with it. It
answers a different question: which predictors explain variance?
The present study asks how accurately the two whole-model \(R^2\)
estimators recover the truth. We have not included \texttt{partR2} in
the empirical comparison for this reason. It targets
predictor-level attribution, not the whole-model \(R^2_m\)/\(R^2_c\)
estimands studied here.

\subsubsection{The Rights-Sterba Integrative Framework}\label{the-rights-sterba-integrative-framework}

\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-rightsQuantifyingExplainedVariance2019}{8}} decompose the model-implied
outcome variance into parts due to level-1 fixed effects, level-2
fixed effects, random-slope variation, random-intercept variation
and residual variation. They show that ten earlier measures
target the same population quantities as five of theirs. Johnson's
extension of Nakagawa and Schielzeth is one of them, and the
equivalence holds in the sample as well. They also note that the
original 2013 partition excluded random-slope variation. The
framework is implemented in the \texttt{r2mlm} package.\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-rightsNewRecommendationsUse2020}{9}} address \(R^2\)
differences between nested models. That is the setting of our
monotonicity aim. We use this framework as the reference point for
interpreting our two estimators. We do not evaluate \texttt{r2mlm}
empirically.

Table \ref{tab:methods-summary} places all six approaches in

That is, they are not merely deferred. Edwards et al.~(2008) and
Jaeger et al.~(2017) target fixed-effects-only estimands with no
conditional-\(R^2\) analogue. As a result, they are not directly
comparable to the marginal/conditional pair studied here. Xu
(2003) needs a custom implementation, since no maintained R
package exists. That is out of scope for a simulation of this
size. The \texttt{performance} package implementation matches our
Johnson estimator in non-singular fits (see previous section).
Including it would therefore add little beyond the two estimators
already compared. All four remain candidates for an
expanded comparison. This would follow if the design and compute
budget in \texttt{docs/plan-full.md} are carried out (see Discussion).

\section{Simulation Design}\label{simulation-design}

The simulation follows the ADEMP framework\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-morrisUsingSimulationStudies2019}{1}}. This framework organizes a
simulation study around five components. These are Aims,
Data-generating mechanisms, Estimands, Methods, and Performance
measures. The structured design follows a broader
recommendation. That is, simulation studies in medical statistics
should be planned and reported as formally as any other empirical
study\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-burtonDesignAnalysisMonteCarlo2006}{19}}.

\subsection{Aims}\label{aims}

The simulation addresses four questions:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\item
  \textbf{Accuracy.} How well does each \(R^2\) method recover the
  true proportion of variance explained (marginal and
  conditional)? We ask this across a range of data-generating
  conditions.
\item
  \textbf{Sensitivity.} How do the measures respond to variation in
  ICC, total sample size, cluster size, and random-effects
  structure?
\item
  \textbf{Monotonicity.} When a true predictor is added to the
  model, does \(R^2\) always increase? And how does \(R^2\) respond
  when a null predictor is added?
\item
  \textbf{Robustness.} How do the measures behave under model
  misspecification? Specifically, what happens when a random
  slope is present in the data but omitted from the fitted model?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Data-Generating Mechanisms}\label{data-generating-mechanisms}

Data are generated from the two-level linear mixed model:

\begin{equation}
y_{ij} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{1ij} + \beta_2 x_{2ij} + b_{0i} +
  b_{1i} x_{1ij} + \varepsilon_{ij}
\label{eq:dgp}
\end{equation}

where \(i = 1, \ldots, J\) indexes clusters and \(j = 1, \ldots, n\)
indexes observations within clusters. The random intercept is
\(b_{0i} \sim N(0, \tau_0^2)\). The random slope is
\(b_{1i} \sim N(0, \tau_1^2)\), set to zero in intercept-only
scenarios. The residual is \(\varepsilon_{ij} \sim N(0, \sigma^2)\),
and all predictors are independent with \(x_k \sim N(0, 1)\).

The variance components are set analytically from two target
quantities, the marginal \(R^2\) and the ICC. We fix
\(\sigma^2 = 1\). The random-intercept variance is then
\(\tau_0^2 = \sigma^2 \cdot \text{ICC} / (1 - \text{ICC})\).
Next, we solve for the fixed-effect coefficients that achieve the
target \(R^2_m\). This means the true \(R^2\) values are known exactly
for each scenario. In random-slope scenarios the ICC factor is
\(\tau_0^2/(\tau_0^2 + \sigma^2)\). That is the intraclass
correlation at \(x_1 = 0\) only. The average clustering is larger.
The slope adds between-cluster variance at other values of
\(x_1\).

In random-slope scenarios, the random-slope variance is fixed at
\(\tau_1 = 0.5 \, \tau_0\) (implemented in
\texttt{R/math\_utilities.R::solve\_dgp\_params()}). That is, the random
slope's SD is half the random intercept's SD. We note that this
is a disclosed design choice. It is not solved for from ICC or
target \(R^2_m\). It fixes a single, moderate slope-to-intercept
variance ratio (\(\tau_1^2 / \tau_0^2 = 0.25\)). As a result,
\texttt{var\_random\ =\ tau0\^{}2\ +\ tau1\^{}2} and the true conditional \(R^2\) are
well-defined and reproducible across scenarios. This is achieved
without a sixth crossed factor. Unfortunately, the ratio was not
varied factorially. Every random-slope result in this manuscript
(Tables \ref{tab:bias-marginal}, \ref{tab:mse-table}, and
\ref{tab:bias-conditional}) is therefore conditional on this one
value. The random-slope conclusions apply to this specific,
moderate ratio. They should not be read as general claims about
arbitrary slope-to-intercept variance ratios. A factorial
sensitivity analysis that varies \(\tau_1/\tau_0\) would be a
natural extension. One choice of levels is
\(\{0.25, 0.5, 1.0\}\). We hope to take this up in future work.

The full factorial design crosses five factors:

This yields \(2^5 = 32\) scenarios. Each is replicated 500 times,
for a total of 16,000 replicate data sets. Each data set receives
three model fits, or four in random-slope scenarios (see Methods
Under Comparison). Each replicate receives a
unique pre-generated seed so that results are reproducible.

\subsection{Estimands}\label{estimands}

The estimands are the \emph{true} marginal and conditional \(R^2\)
values. We compute them analytically from the known variance
components of each scenario:

\begin{equation}
R^2_m = \frac{\text{Var}(X\beta)}{\text{Var}(X\beta) +
  \text{Var}(\text{random}) + \sigma^2}
\label{eq:true-r2m}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
R^2_c = \frac{\text{Var}(X\beta) + \text{Var}(\text{random})}{
  \text{Var}(X\beta) + \text{Var}(\text{random}) + \sigma^2}
\label{eq:true-r2c}
\end{equation}

Here \(\text{Var}(\text{random}) = \tau_0^2 + \tau_1^2\). That
follows from \(x_1\) having mean 0 and variance 1. This is the
population value of
Johnson's term in Equation \eqref{eq:johnson}.

\subsection{Methods Under Comparison}\label{methods-under-comparison}

Both estimators are computed directly from the fitted \texttt{lme4}
model (\texttt{R/compute\_r2.R}). Each uses the same plug-in fixed-effect
variance, \(\hat\sigma^2_f = \widehat{\text{Var}}(X\hat\beta)\), and
the same residual variance \(\hat\sigma^2\). They differ only in
the random-effect variance.

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Nakagawa-Schielzeth (2013):} the original formula. The
  random-effect variance is \(\hat\tau_0^2\) alone. Any slope
  variance is ignored. This is what an analyst obtains by
  applying the 2013 formula to a random-slope fit.
\item
  \textbf{Johnson (2014):} the random-effect variance is Equation
  \eqref{eq:johnson}, evaluated at the estimated \(\hat\Sigma\) and
  the observed \(x_1\).
\end{itemize}

The two estimators are identical for random-intercept models. So
the comparison is informative only in the 16 random-slope
scenarios. As an implementation check, every full-model fit is
also passed to \texttt{MuMIn::r.squaredGLMM()}\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-bartonMuMIn2024}{12}}. We
record the largest absolute difference from our Johnson estimate.

We add one exploratory comparator for \(R^2_m\). The plug-in
\(\hat\sigma^2_f\) is biased upward. To first order its expectation
is \(\beta^\top S_x \beta + \text{tr}\{\text{Var}(\hat\beta) S_x\}\).
Here \(S_x\) is the sample covariance of the predictors. The
bias-adjusted estimator subtracts the estimated trace term from
\(\hat\sigma^2_f\), truncates at zero, and uses Johnson's
random-effect variance. It is not a published estimator. We
include it to test whether the trace term explains the observed
bias.

Each replicate fits up to four models to the same data set:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  the \emph{full} model, with \(x_1\), \(x_2\) and the generating
  random-effects structure;
\item
  a \emph{reduced} model with \(x_1\) only, to test monotonicity when a
  true predictor is added;
\item
  a \emph{null-augmented} model with \(x_1\), \(x_2\) and a pure-noise
  predictor \(x_3 \sim N(0, 1)\), to test monotonicity when a null
  predictor is added;
\item
  in random-slope scenarios, a \emph{misspecified} model with
  \(x_1\), \(x_2\) and a random intercept only (Aim 4).
\end{itemize}

All fits use the default correlated
\texttt{(1\ +\ x1\ \textbar{}\ cluster\_id)} structure when a slope is fitted. The
null predictor is drawn after all other quantities. So it leaves
the response and true predictors unchanged for a given seed.

\subsection{Performance Measures}\label{performance-measures}

Following\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-morrisUsingSimulationStudies2019}{1}}, we report:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Bias:} \(\bar{\hat{R}}^2 - R^2_{\text{true}}\)
\item
  \textbf{Relative bias:} Bias / \(R^2_{\text{true}}\)
\item
  \textbf{Empirical SE:} SD(\(\hat{R}^2\)) over replicates
\item
  \textbf{Mean squared error (MSE):} \(E[(\hat{R}^2 - R^2_{\text{true}})^2]\)
\item
  \textbf{Monotonicity rate:} Proportion of replicates where
  \(\hat{R}^2_{\text{larger}} \geq \hat{R}^2_{\text{smaller}}\),
  for both \(R^2_m\) and \(R^2_c\), when a true or a null predictor
  is added
\item
  \textbf{Singular-fit and convergence-warning rates:} Proportion of
  full-model fits that \texttt{lme4} flags as singular, or for which it
  reports a convergence warning
\item
  \textbf{Monte Carlo standard errors (MCSE):} For bias, MSE and
  the rates above
\end{itemize}

We do not report a boundary-violation rate. Every estimator here
is a ratio of non-negative variance estimates. So it cannot fall
outside {[}0, 1{]}.

\section{Results}\label{results}

\subsection{Convergence}\label{convergence}

Every one of the 16,000
full-model fits returned a model object without error. This is
not a convergence diagnostic by itself. We therefore also
recorded whether \texttt{lme4} issued a convergence warning (a failed
gradient, Hessian or eigenvalue check). Such warnings occurred in
1 of the full-model fits (0.01\%). These fits were
retained. Overall, 12.3\% of full-model fits were
flagged as singular. The rate was 2.4\%
for random-intercept fits and 22.3\% for
random-slope fits. It peaked at 70.0\% in
scenario 25 (\(J = 20\),
\(n = 5\), ICC 0.1,
slope). Small variance components are hard to
estimate from limited data. Singular fits were retained in all
analyses.

\subsection{Implementation Check and Estimator Identity}\label{implementation-check-and-estimator-identity}

Our Johnson estimate agreed with \texttt{MuMIn::r.squaredGLMM()} in every
full-model fit. The largest absolute difference was
\(5.8 \times 10^{-15}\). The Nakagawa-Schielzeth and Johnson
estimates coincided in all random-intercept fits. The largest
difference there was
exactly zero. They differed in all but 41 of the
8,000 random-slope fits. All
41 of those exceptions were singular fits. This
is consistent with a zero estimated slope variance. We did not
record that variance. Over all
random-slope fits the median difference,
Nakagawa-Schielzeth minus Johnson, was
0.0073 for \(R^2_m\) and
-0.0180 for \(R^2_c\).

\subsection{Bias}\label{bias}

Figure \ref{fig:fig-bias-heatmap} shows the bias in marginal
\(R^2\) across all 32 scenarios. Table \ref{tab:bias-marginal} gives
the numbers with MCSEs and relative bias. The Johnson \(R^2_m\) was
biased upward in 29 of 32 scenarios. The bias exceeded two
MCSEs in 15 of them. Relative bias was largest in scenario
17 (\(J = 20\),
\(n = 5\), ICC 0.1, target
\(R^2_m\) 0.1, slope). There
it reached 11.9\%. With \(N = 100\)
observations and target \(R^2_m = 0.10\), relative bias ranged from
8.6\% to 11.9\%. With
\(N = 1000\) it was at most 3.8\% in absolute value.
So the upward bias is practically relevant only when the sample
is small and the true \(R^2_m\) is small.

The direction and the dependence on \(N\) have a simple source. The
plug-in \(\widehat{\text{Var}}(X\hat\beta)\) includes the sampling
variance of \(\hat\beta\). To first order it overstates
\(\text{Var}(X\beta)\) by \(\text{tr}\{\text{Var}(\hat\beta) S_x\}\).
This term shrinks like \(1/N\). The bias-adjusted estimator removes
this term. Its bias was at most 0.0072
in absolute value. It exceeded two MCSEs in 3 of 32
scenarios. In the 15 scenarios where the Johnson bias
exceeded two MCSEs, the adjusted bias was smaller in
15. The adjustment over-corrects, however. Its bias
was negative in 21 of 32 scenarios. With \(N = 100\) it
ranged from -0.0072 to
-0.0001. It was larger in absolute value
than the Johnson bias in 7 scenarios. The trace term is
therefore a plausible main source of the upward bias. A first-order
correction does not remove it cleanly in the smallest samples.

In random-slope scenarios the original Nakagawa-Schielzeth
\(R^2_m\) was biased upward further. Its relative bias ranged from
1.4\% to 19.0\%, against at most
11.9\% for Johnson. This extra bias does not shrink
with \(N\). Its source is the omitted slope variance in the
denominator.

\subsection{Monotonicity}\label{monotonicity}

Figure \ref{fig:fig-monotonicity} and Table
\ref{tab:mono-table} report monotonicity rates. When the true
predictor \(x_2\) was added, \(\hat R^2_m\) increased in at least
97.8\% of replicates in every scenario. This
comparison is easy by design. The added predictor carries half of
the fixed-effect variance. The conditional \(R^2\) was less
reliable. The Johnson \(\hat R^2_c\) increased in only
87.2\% of replicates in scenario
17. Its rate fell below 95\% only when
\(J = 20\) and \(n = 5\).

The null-predictor arm is more informative. Adding a pure-noise
predictor raised the Johnson \(\hat R^2_m\) in only
47.2\% to 77.2\% of replicates. The
mean rate was 74.2\% at target
\(R^2_m = 0.10\) and 51.1\% at 0.40. For
\(\hat R^2_c\) the rate ranged from 39.0\% to
61.4\%. Unlike \(R^2\) in least squares, neither
estimator is monotone in the set of fixed effects. One plausible
reason is that the residual and random-effect variances are
re-estimated with each added term. We have not verified this
mechanism.
A small increase or decrease in \(\hat R^2_m\) after adding a
predictor is not by itself evidence for or against that
predictor. This agrees with the caution of\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-rightsNewRecommendationsUse2020}{9}} about \(R^2\) differences in
multilevel model comparison.

\subsection{Robustness to an Omitted Random Slope}\label{robustness-to-an-omitted-random-slope}

In the 16 random-slope scenarios we also fitted a
random-intercept-only model (Aim 4). The marginal \(R^2\) from this
misspecified fit stayed close to the truth. Its bias was at most
0.0122 in absolute value. Its MSE was
0.98 to
1.11 times that of the Johnson
estimate from the correct fit. The bias of the conditional \(R^2\)
ranged from -0.085 to
-0.005. At ICC 0.40 the range was
-0.085 to
-0.050. This pattern is
consistent with the slope variance being absorbed into the
residual variance. We did not check this directly. These
results hold for our slope-to-intercept ratio,
\(\tau_1 = 0.5\,\tau_0\).

\section{Discussion}\label{discussion}

The original Nakagawa-Schielzeth formula and the Johnson
extension coincide for random-intercept models. This is true by
construction. The simulation adds nothing on that point. In
random-slope models they differ. The original formula ignores the
slope variance. As a result it overstates \(R^2_m\) and understates
\(R^2_c\). In our design the error did not shrink as \(N\) grew. The
Johnson extension removes this error. Current \texttt{MuMIn} applies the
Johnson term to every random-slope fit. So users of \texttt{MuMIn}
already obtain it. The original formula remains a risk for hand-coded or
older implementations.

The Johnson \(\hat R^2_m\) is biased upward in small samples. The
bias is largest when \(N\) and the true \(R^2_m\) are both small. The
source is the sampling variance of \(\hat\beta\) inside
\(\widehat{\text{Var}}(X\hat\beta)\). Subtracting the trace term
reduced the bias where it was large. It over-corrected slightly
when \(N = 100\). We regard this adjusted estimator as exploratory.
Its variance and its behavior with
correlated predictors have not been studied here.

Several further patterns warrant attention. First, small samples
(\(J = 20\), \(n = 5\)) produced wider, right-skewed sampling
distributions. So \(R^2\) estimates from small multilevel studies
should be interpreted with caution. Second, neither \(R^2_m\) nor
\(R^2_c\) is monotone when a null predictor is added. Third, the
effect of ICC on precision depends on the target \(R^2_m\). Fourth,
singular fits were common in low-ICC random-slope scenarios. This
is a well-known limitation of maximum likelihood estimation for
small variance components\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-batesLme4LinearMixed2015}{4}}.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Directions}\label{limitations-and-future-directions}

This study compared two estimators from one family. Natural
candidates for an expanded comparison are the Edwards et al.
statistic and the Jaeger et al.~semi-partial decomposition. Xu's
measures and the Rights-Sterba measures in \texttt{r2mlm} are others. The
reason for excluding each from the present empirical comparison
is stated in the `Summary of Methods' section above.

The random-slope conclusions in this study (Results, Discussion)
rest on a single, disclosed slope-to-intercept variance ratio,
\(\tau_1 = 0.5\,\tau_0\) (see Data-Generating Mechanisms). This
ratio was not varied factorially. The conclusions should not be
read as general claims across arbitrary random-slope variance
structures.

The fitted models use the default correlated
random-intercept/random-slope parameterization in \texttt{lme4}. That
is, they fit \texttt{(1\ +\ x1\ \textbar{}\ cluster\_id)}, an unstructured \(G\) matrix.
The data-generating process, by contrast, draws the random
intercept and random slope independently (\texttt{R/generate\_data.R}).
This mismatch reflects standard applied practice. Most analysts
fit an unstructured \(G\) matrix without first testing whether the
true intercept-slope correlation is zero. However, it means the
fitted models estimate one more variance-covariance parameter
than the DGP requires. This plausibly contributes to the elevated
singular-fit rate in low-ICC random-slope scenarios (Results,
Convergence). We did not fit the uncorrelated
\texttt{(1\ +\ x1\ \textbar{}\textbar{}\ cluster\_id)} structure as a sensitivity arm. That is a
cheap extension for a later revision.

The present design is restricted to balanced clusters and
Gaussian outcomes. Several extensions would increase the
practical relevance of the findings. These include unbalanced
designs, binary or count outcomes (GLMMs), and crossed random
effects. The DGP also assumed independent predictors. Correlated
predictors could change the relative performance of two kinds of
method. Some decompose variance at the predictor level (Jaeger et
al.). Others operate on the total fixed-effect variance
(Nakagawa-Schielzeth, Johnson).

Finally, this study does not report coverage of confidence or
credible intervals for \(\hat{R}^2\).\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-morrisUsingSimulationStudies2019}{1}} recommend this performance
measure, and it is standard in \(R^2\)-estimator evaluations. We
omit it here for a practical reason. Neither estimator comes with
an analytic standard error or interval for \(R^2_m\)/\(R^2_c\).
Constructing one would need a parametric bootstrap, as in
\texttt{partR2}\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-stoffelPartR2Partitioning2021}{11}}, or a delta-method
addition to \texttt{R/compute\_r2.R}. We judged this out of scope for the
present comparison. The coverage of a bootstrap interval is a
concrete target for a future revision.

\subsection{Practical Recommendations}\label{practical-recommendations}

On the basis of these results, we offer the following guidance
for applied researchers:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\item
  For random-intercept models, the original Nakagawa-Schielzeth
  formula and the Johnson extension are the same estimator.
  Either name may be used.
\item
  For random-slope models, use the Johnson extension. The
  original formula ignores the slope variance. In our design it
  overstated \(R^2_m\) and understated \(R^2_c\). The error did not
  shrink with sample size. Current \texttt{MuMIn::r.squaredGLMM()}
  already applies the Johnson term. Its \texttt{method} argument does
  not change the result for Gaussian models.
\item
  Both marginal and conditional \(R^2\) should be reported. They
  answer distinct questions about population-average versus
  cluster-specific prediction.
\item
  In small-sample settings (\(J < 30\) or \(n < 10\)), \(R^2\)
  estimates should be accompanied by measures of uncertainty. A
  parametric-bootstrap interval, as provided by \texttt{partR2}\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-stoffelPartR2Partitioning2021}{11}}, is one option. Monte Carlo
  standard errors are not suitable here. They measure simulation
  error in a performance estimate, not the uncertainty of an
  estimate from one data set. We have not evaluated the coverage
  of any such interval. Expect \(\hat R^2_m\) to run somewhat high
  when both \(N\) and the true \(R^2_m\) are small.
\item
  Do not read a small change in \(R^2_m\) or \(R^2_c\) after adding
  a predictor as evidence about that predictor. Either estimate
  can fall when a true predictor is added in small samples. It
  can rise or fall when a null predictor is added.
\item
  Convergence diagnostics should always be checked. A singular
  fit indicates that the random-effects structure may be
  over-parameterized for the available data. \(R^2\) estimates
  from such fits should be interpreted cautiously.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusions}\label{conclusions}

We have evaluated the plug-in marginal and conditional \(R^2\) for
Gaussian LMMs in a small factorial design with known truth. We
compared the original Nakagawa-Schielzeth formula with Johnson's
random-slope extension. The two coincide for random-intercept
models. For random-slope models the original formula is biased in
both \(R^2_m\) and \(R^2_c\), and the Johnson extension corrects this.
The Johnson \(\hat R^2_m\) itself is biased upward in small samples.
The sampling variance of \(\hat\beta\) is a plausible main source
of this bias.
Neither estimator is monotone when a null predictor is added.
Omitting a random slope left \(R^2_m\) largely intact but biased
\(R^2_c\) downward. In sum, the results underscore the importance of
adequate sample sizes for reliable \(R^2\) estimation in multilevel
designs. They also support the routine reporting of both marginal
and conditional \(R^2\), alongside convergence diagnostics.

\textbf{Random-number generator.} \texttt{analysis/scripts/simulation.R}
pins \texttt{RNGkind("L\textquotesingle{}Ecuyer-CMRG")} once at program start, before any
seeding. This follows Morris et al.~(2019) Section 4.1.
Per-replicate seeds are not derived from a running counter.
Instead, \texttt{create\_scenario\_grid()} (\texttt{R/generate\_data.R}) calls
\texttt{set.seed(master\_seed)} once, with \texttt{master\_seed\ =\ 20260306}. It
then draws one seed per scenario-replicate via
\texttt{sample.int(.Machine\$integer.max,\ total\_reps)}. As a result, all
16,000 replicate-level seeds are pre-generated. They are stored
in the scenario grid before any model fitting begins. Each stored
seed is used exactly once, by \texttt{set.seed(seed)} inside
\texttt{generate\_lmm\_data()} (\texttt{R/generate\_data.R}). This call comes
immediately before that replicate's data are drawn. Parallel
execution across replicates within a scenario uses
\texttt{furrr::future\_map\_dfr()} with \texttt{furrr\_options(seed\ =\ NULL)}
(\texttt{R/simulation.R::run\_scenario()}). We note that \texttt{seed\ =\ NULL} is
appropriate here and is not a gap in the seeding strategy. The
RNG state is already reproducible through the pre-drawn,
per-replicate \texttt{set.seed()} call described above. The
parallel-safe seeding layer in \texttt{furrr} would therefore be
redundant. Each worker first sets the RNG kind of the calling
session. So a replicate gives the same data under parallel and
sequential execution. We confirmed this on three replicates. To
reproduce a specific replicate, the reader should
regenerate the scenario grid with
\texttt{create\_scenario\_grid(master\_seed\ =\ 20260306)}. The replicate's
seed can then be read from its \texttt{seed} column. It should not be
computed as \texttt{master\_seed\ +\ rep\_idx}.

\section{Morris et al.~(2019) ADEMP Compliance}\label{morris-et-al.-2019-ademp-compliance}

We audited this simulation study against the reporting standards
proposed by Morris, White, and Crowther\textsuperscript{\citeproc{ref-morrisUsingSimulationStudies2019}{1}}. This section reports the
audit as re-run against the code that produced the results in
this manuscript. It supersedes the earlier
\texttt{docs/morris-audit-2026-04-17.md} snapshot. Three of the four gaps
listed in that snapshot are now closed in the current code. The
November 2026 revision also added a convergence-warning flag to
\texttt{fit\_lmm()}. Previously the reported convergence rate was true by
construction.

\textbf{Verdict:} Mostly compliant.

\textbf{Gaps closed since the prior audit:}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \texttt{n\_sim\ =\ 500} is documented with a Monte Carlo SE justification
  in \texttt{analysis/scripts/simulation.R}. The observed empirical SE
  of the Johnson \(\hat R^2_m\) ranged from
  0.016 to
  0.079 across
  scenarios. At \(n_{sim} = 500\) the largest bias MCSE is therefore
  0.0035, in the
  worst cell. This is adequate to detect the biases discussed in
  Results.
\item
  \texttt{RNGkind("L\textquotesingle{}Ecuyer-CMRG")} is now pinned at the top of
  \texttt{analysis/scripts/simulation.R}, before any seeding. The same
  RNG kind is now also set inside each parallel worker
  (\texttt{R/simulation.R::run\_scenario()}). Before this fix, workers
  used R's default generator. The same seed then gave different
  data under parallel and sequential runs.
\item
  Per-replicate RNG states. The snapshot asked for \texttt{.Random.seed}
  to be stored after each replicate. Here each replicate calls
  \texttt{set.seed()} on its own pre-drawn seed inside
  \texttt{generate\_lmm\_data()}. Its starting state is therefore fixed by
  that seed and the RNG kind. No state carries over from the
  previous replicate. We checked this directly.
  \texttt{analysis/scripts/check\_seed\_reproduction.R} regenerated
  64 replicates (the first and last of
  each of the 32 scenarios) from their
  seeds alone. All 17 stored \(R^2\) columns
  matched exactly (maximum absolute difference
  0). From the
  next full run, \texttt{generate\_simulated\_results()} also writes each
  replicate's seed into the results file. The current
  \texttt{sim\_results.rds} predates that change. Its seeds are recovered
  from \texttt{create\_scenario\_grid()} and the master seed.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Gap still open:}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Coverage is not among the reported performance measures (see
  Limitations for the reason and a proposed remediation).
\end{itemize}

A remediation plan for the open gap is recorded in
\texttt{docs/morris-audit-2026-04-17.md}. We shall take it up in a
subsequent revision.
